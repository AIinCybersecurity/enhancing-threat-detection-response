Overview

Abstract

This research project investigates the use of artificial intelligence (AI) in detecting and preventing cyber threats, with a particular focus on the Bahrain context. Cyberattacks are becoming increasingly complex, and manual methods simply are not keeping up. This is why it has become extremely crucial to begin using smarter, automated tools.

Two approaches were used: reviewing a substantial body of existing research and conducting a survey with 250 people, primarily IT students and professionals. The study examined whether people are aware of AI tools, whether they believe they are dependable, and what prompts a company to use these tools.

Results suggest that most respondents hold moderately positive views of AI. The average attitude score was 3.64 out of 5. Approximately 60% reported that AI is significant in cybersecurity right now — though concerns remain about accuracy and over-reliance. Findings demonstrate that AI is most effective when used as an adjunct to human expertise rather than in isolation.

Chapter 1

Introduction

1.1 Research Background

This section introduces the basics of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in cybersecurity, facilitating an understanding of AI's real-life applications in digital threat detection and response. This background provides readers with context for appreciating the current study and comparing survey results against the real-world experiences of IT professionals and students.

1.1.1 Overview of Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity is described by NIST (2023) as the process of protecting computers, systems, networks, and data from attacks or damage, thereby ensuring uninterrupted functioning and safety. With the majority of organizations — including financial institutions, healthcare facilities, educational institutions, and governmental bodies — utilizing computers for various purposes, cybersecurity has become one of the most important tasks within IT.

The primary objective of cybersecurity is to keep the CIA triad: Confidentiality (data kept private), Integrity (data not altered without authorization), and Availability (systems and data available when required). Cyber threats have evolved and become harder to manage. Phishing attacks, malware, ransomware, and large-scale data breaches are becoming more widespread and damaging, driving organizations to search for AI-powered cybersecurity solutions.

1.1.2 Evolution of Artificial Intelligence

Early AI systems were mostly rule-based: they had to be programmed with all the logic they required to execute. They failed when there was anything new or unexpected. Machine learning changed that — enabling systems to learn on their own through data rather than following a fixed set of instructions. Deep learning followed, utilizing neural networks to process highly complex data such as images, speech, or large amounts of network traffic.

In the cybersecurity world, this translates to a shift from simply recognizing signature-based threats to learning and adapting. Older systems could only detect familiar attacks, whereas newer AI systems can detect unusual patterns even if they have never encountered that attack before.

1.1.3 Role of AI in Cybersecurity

AI is currently used for several specific jobs in cybersecurity. The most apparent is threat detection: AI systems can more quickly and effectively process large amounts of data and identify anything suspicious. AI is also involved in incident response — once a threat is verified, AI can help determine how dangerous it is, which systems are affected, and in some instances automatically respond by isolating compromised devices or blocking suspicious traffic. Additionally, AI helps security teams reduce false alarms by removing noise and narrowing focus to things that truly matter.

1.2 Research Problem

Problem Statement: Cybersecurity threats have become more sophisticated, faster, and harder to detect using traditional defence mechanisms. Conventional security systems often rely on static rules, manual monitoring, and reactive responses, making them insufficient against modern attacks such as zero-day exploits, advanced persistent threats (APTs), and large-scale data breaches. While AI offers promising capabilities — real-time threat detection, automated response, and predictive analysis — its integration into cybersecurity is not without challenges. Organizations face uncertainties regarding accuracy, reliability, and ethical implications, as well as concerns about over-reliance on automation.

This research seeks to examine the core issues surrounding the adoption and effectiveness of AI in cybersecurity, aiming to:

  • Identify the factors that influence the successful implementation of AI in threat detection and response systems.
  • Assess the impact of these factors on the overall performance, reliability, and decision-making quality of AI-driven cybersecurity solutions.

1.3 Research Questions

  • What are the factors affecting the effective adoption of AI as a threat detection and response tool in cybersecurity?
  • How do these factors influence the capability of AI systems to detect, analyse, and respond to cyber threats?
  • How do organizational knowledge, security team attitudes, perceived usefulness, and perceived ease of use affect the intention to adopt AI-based cybersecurity solutions?
  • How does organizational background, the nature of cybersecurity environments, security preferences, and exposure to cyber threats influence the intention to implement AI technologies?

1.4 Research Objectives

  • Understand how modern AI and machine learning improve the speed and accuracy of detecting cyber threats compared to older methods.
  • Explore how AI can help respond to attacks in real time, including learning from new threats as they happen.
  • Study ways to make AI's decisions more transparent and understandable for security teams.
  • Identify challenges like attackers trying to fool AI systems and find ways to make AI-based security stronger and more dependable.

1.5 Research Significance

This research is significant because cyberattacks have increased in frequency and severity. Existing systems rely on fixed signatures or rules, which does not work well against new types of attacks. AI-based IDS systems can process vast amounts of network traffic and system logs faster than human counterparts, detecting suspicious activity faster and providing security personnel more time to respond. AI can identify both known attacks and unknown attack types by learning normal behaviors on a network.

Furthermore, AI helps to speed up incident response. The automated response system can quickly contain threats by isolating affected systems or blocking malicious traffic before severe damage is done. This research adds to the body of knowledge by examining both the technical and human aspects of AI's implementation in cybersecurity, providing guidance for Bahrain and other Gulf region organizations.

1.6 Gaps Analysis

While there is significant research around AI in cybersecurity, several gaps remain:

  • Many studies focus on enhancing detection accuracy using ML/DL methods but fail to consider practical problems of deployment within an organization's environment.
  • There is a lack of explainability and trust — existing literature on interpretability remains abstract without practical solutions.
  • Most studies view threat detection and incident response as two distinct entities, with little research examining AI in an integrated workflow including detection, response, and continuous learning.
  • Insufficient research focuses on human and organizational issues: willingness of security teams to embrace AI, training requirements, and whether they trust the tools they use.

1.7 Theoretical Framework

1.7.1 AI-Driven Threat Detection Models

Traditional security tools rely on a rule or known signature and are poorly able to defend against attacks they have never seen. AI uses machine learning and deep learning algorithms to identify abnormal or harmful activity in systems, networks, and user behavior, even when that activity does not match any previously known pattern.

Supervised learning algorithms like Support Vector Machines or Random Forest classify network traffic as normal or malicious. Deep learning techniques like Deep Neural Networks and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) can establish more intricate patterns and abnormalities concealed in massive datasets. Anomaly detection uses unsupervised learning methods such as clustering and auto-encoders — useful for detecting new attack types because they do not require labeled attacks to function. AI can also aggregate information from various sources through data fusion, providing a more comprehensive understanding of network events.

Challenges include imbalanced datasets, biased models, and the complexity of deep learning systems, which is why joining AI analysis and human review has been recommended by many researchers.

1.7.2 Incident Response Frameworks

Incident response encompasses formal stages: Preparation, Detection, Containment, Eradication, Recovery, and Post-incident Review. With the inclusion of AI, all of these can be quicker and more accurate. This theoretical model is based on the NIST Incident Response Life Cycle.

Organizations are incorporating SOAR (Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response) into their incident response strategies. SOAR tools, when integrated with AI, can correlate security alerts from different sources, provide context, and suggest or carry out remediation measures — minimizing critical performance indicators like MTTD and MTTR. A continuous learning loop allows data from resolved incidents to refine the AI system for future detection and response.

The framework acknowledges the importance of people's participation: while AI processes vast amounts of data and repetitive monitoring tasks, human analysts ensure AI is robust and interprets intricate situations appropriately.

1.8 Research Methodology

1.8.1 Data Collection

(a) Primary data: Google Forms were used to design an online questionnaire distributed via digital means. Closed-ended questions with a five-point Likert-type scale gauged participants' awareness of AI use in cybersecurity and their confidence in automated defense systems.

(b) Secondary data: Academic journals and conference papers published from 2016 to 2025 were reviewed via IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, and SpringerLink. Key areas included Machine Learning-based Intrusion Detection, Automated Response Systems, and Explainable AI in Cybersecurity.

1.8.2 Data Analysis

Survey answers were transformed into facts: background questions compiled into percentages, Likert-scale questions calculated as average scores, and charts created to make patterns easy to see. Literature themes were compared to questionnaire results to understand both how AI works technically and how respondents feel about using it in their daily lives.

1.8.3 Ethical Considerations

All data were collected ethically. Participation in the survey was voluntary and anonymous, and no personal or sensitive information was recorded. All secondary sources were properly cited following academic standards.

Chapter 2

Literature Review

2.1 Cybersecurity

2.1.1 Definition of Cybersecurity

As defined by NIST (2023), cybersecurity refers to the processes adopted to safeguard computers, computer networks, and any information stored within such devices against unauthorized access, destruction, or disruption. Core concepts include confidentiality (keeping data confidential), integrity (protecting data from unauthorized modification), and availability (ensuring accessibility of data). Importantly, cybersecurity goes beyond technology — it includes both policy and behavior within organizations.

2.1.2 Importance of Cybersecurity

The significance of cybersecurity has increased tremendously because most organizations now depend on technology to store critical information and conduct business. A cyber attack can lead to devastating effects including losses, destruction, and legal ramifications. Health organizations, financial institutions, educational organizations, and government agencies are at the highest risk due to the value of their information. A poor security system makes it relatively simple to steal valuable data or cause irreparable damage to IT infrastructure, leading to a loss of customer and partner confidence.

2.1.3 Common Cybersecurity Threats

Common cybersecurity threats include:

  • Malware — malicious programs used to corrupt systems, steal information, or break into networks.
  • Phishing — deceptive techniques using fake communications to steal credentials or sensitive data.
  • DoS/DDoS attacks — flooding a server with requests so regular clients cannot connect.
  • Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs) — qualified professionals who enter networks and remain for months to collect and extract information secretly.
  • Insider threats — caused by user actions, either intentional attacks or accidental errors.

2.1.4 Incident Response in Cybersecurity

Incident response refers to the sequential approach employed by organizations in reaction to a detected cyberattack. Per NIST (2023), the procedure involves six stages: Preparation, Detection and Analysis, Containment, Eradication, Recovery, and Post-incident Activity. This set of actions is critical for minimizing harm while restoring operational capabilities promptly. Successful incident response requires both security technologies and human decision-makers in collaborative action. The reliance on manual intervention leads to delays, particularly in cases of massive or intricate attacks — which is why AI-driven and automated incident response procedures are increasingly important.

2.1.5 Traditional Cybersecurity Approaches

Conventional security software relied heavily on signature-based detection, which can only identify malware if it is preprogrammed into its database. If hackers develop new malware or modify existing malware, conventional tools cannot detect it. Additionally, traditional tools are known for excessive alerting — security personnel become fatigued after receiving thousands of false alerts daily, making it difficult to pay attention to real attacks. Furthermore, conventional tools lack interoperability, making it challenging to coordinate security team efforts across an entire infrastructure.

2.2 Artificial Intelligence (AI)

2.2.1 Definition and Overview of AI

Artificial Intelligence refers to programs written for computers to perform tasks that would normally be accomplished by humans, such as problem solving, reasoning, and pattern recognition. Most AI used nowadays belongs to a category known as narrow AI — a particular application that can complete a predetermined task extremely effectively. The key difference between AI and ordinary software is the ability to improve through practice: ordinary programs follow fixed commands, while AI programs constantly update their knowledge base by acquiring new data and modifying how they operate.

2.2.2 History of Artificial Intelligence

Early AI was very basic and ran on predefined rule sets — it could not handle situations outside its programmed logic. The emergence of machine learning enabled computers to identify patterns in data without being explicitly programmed to do so. Presently, the process is more advanced through deep learning, which uses artificial neural networks that mimic some cognitive functions in humans to interpret images, text, and network traffic analysis.

2.2.3 AI Tools and Techniques

AI approaches include:

  • Supervised learning — uses pre-labeled data to derive conclusions.
  • Unsupervised learning — explores data and discovers patterns automatically.
  • Reinforcement learning — the computer learns through experiments, with a reward given when doing something right.
  • Deep learning — uses neural networks (CNNs, RNNs) to work with highly sophisticated data.
  • Natural language processing and expert systems — used in various cybersecurity applications.

2.2.4 Applications of AI in Different Fields

AI is incorporated across many industries: in medicine for disease determination and health monitoring; in finance for fraud detection, loan eligibility, and risk assessment; in transportation for traffic management and self-driving cars; and in manufacturing for predictive maintenance. In cybersecurity, AI plays an essential role in detecting potential threats, analyzing network behaviors, and stopping cyberattacks automatically. It helps cybersecurity experts manage enormous amounts of data daily.

2.2.5 Factors Impacting the Effectiveness of AI

For an AI model to be effective: (1) Data quality is critical — a lot of unbiased data needs to be fed into the model so that it learns to produce reliable output; corrupted data will lead to a biased model. (2) Transparency in AI systems — many deep learning models are referred to as "black boxes" because the reasons for their decisions are hard to comprehend, creating mistrust in areas like cybersecurity where reliability is key. Other factors include computing power requirements and the skills of people running the systems.

2.3 Integration of AI in Cybersecurity

2.3.1 Relationship between AI and Cybersecurity

AI and cybersecurity are linked concepts due to the massive amount of data produced by computers every day — including logs of internet traffic and users' actions. AI technologies are adept at processing such data because algorithms can detect hidden trends and unusual behavior. One of the strengths of using AI for cybersecurity is its ability to adapt to changes: traditional security methods use static rules, while AI-based security can learn from each threat and update its defenses accordingly. Precautions must ensure AI is not used by adversaries for malicious purposes, and that decisions remain transparent.

2.3.2 AI for Enhancing Threat Detection

AI systems perform better in detecting threats since they use data-driven approaches to detect hazardous patterns regardless of whether the type of attack has occurred before. AI can simultaneously analyze old and current data to detect if network traffic is legitimate or malicious. Deep learning can find increasingly subtle patterns in large datasets to improve detection results in high-traffic networks. Anomaly detection develops a model of how typical network traffic should look, setting up alerts whenever deviation occurs — effective for detecting insider threats and zero-day attacks. AI can also reduce false positives by incorporating information from different security sources.

2.3.3 Incident Response and Threat Analysis using AI

AI extends beyond threat detection to help companies address problems associated with breaches and implement solutions. AI assists in determining the scope of security incidents, correlating incidents to network activities, and implementing automatic actions like blocking malicious traffic and isolating affected systems. AI is also extremely helpful in retrospective analysis and understanding the root causes of intrusions — vital for developing preventive actions. Nevertheless, human involvement remains crucial for verifying and checking AI decisions for correctness.

2.3.4 Technical Factors Influencing AI-based Threat Detection

Important technical considerations include: (1) Data quality — large amounts of reliable data are required; inaccurate data results in flawed conclusions and high false positive rates. (2) Model complexity — more complex deep-learning algorithms may have better detection results but require significant computing resources and may be difficult for security experts to understand. (3) Robustness — the system must resist manipulation attempts by adversaries who try to mislead the AI with carefully chosen inputs. Appropriate training and constant updating of the model are essential.

2.3.5 Future Trends in AI-driven Cybersecurity

Current research focuses on Explainable AI to make the rationale behind AI computations comprehensible to security experts. Another emerging area is predictive AI, using past data to predict when and where future attacks might occur. Scientists are also improving AI algorithms for cloud computing and network security. Challenges include privacy, discrimination, biased algorithms, and the use of AI by adversaries for more advanced attacks — fueling what researchers describe as a "digital arms race." Overall, AI is viewed as the best solution for increasing security, but must be carefully regulated to ensure safety and ethics.

Chapter 3

Research Model and Hypotheses

3.1 Research Model

This research is based on a model consisting of three main variable types:

  • Three independent variables (technical AI capabilities): AI threat detection, AI incident response, and general technical characteristics of AI.
  • One dependent variable: Effectiveness of cybersecurity threat detection and response.
  • Three moderating variables (human and organizational factors): Organizational knowledge and skills, perceived usefulness, and perceived ease of use.

3.1.1 Conceptual Research Framework

The central assumption postulates that AI can improve the security situation significantly — helping teams identify risks faster, responding to attacks more quickly, and maintaining efficiency despite difficult circumstances. However, the relationship between AI and security is affected by organizational factors. As Venkatesh et al. (2016) and Capuano et al. (2022) note, AI will deliver results effectively only when workers have been professionally trained and the usefulness and usability of AI tools are clear to the team.

3.1.2.1 AI-based Threat Detection Capabilities

This variable refers to the ability of AI-powered devices to recognize cyber threats using data analysis. AI uses supervised learning to categorize traffic as safe or malicious, deep learning to uncover hidden threats in big data, and anomaly detection to develop behavioral baselines. An increase in the ability of AI technology to detect attacks leads to lower instances of false negatives and false positives.

3.1.2.2 AI-based Incident Response Capabilities

This variable refers to the ability of AI systems to help or even react on their own when faced with a threat, including ranking alerts by importance, correlating incidents, and performing automatic actions such as removing infiltrated hosts or blocking suspicious traffic. This leads to improvement in speed and effectiveness, which is critical for ensuring minimal damage and enabling swift return to normalcy.

3.1.2.3 Technical Factors of AI Systems

Technical factors form the basis of an effective AI-based security tool, including data quality and accuracy, scalability, adversarial robustness, and seamless integration into existing security architecture. AI systems are only as good as the data they receive, and they must be able to withstand adversarial attacks that attempt to manipulate model outputs.

3.1.3 Dependent Variable

The dependent variable is the Effectiveness of Cybersecurity Threat Detection and Response — the level of capability within a firm to detect cyber threats and effectively react to security threats to avoid damage and return to regular operations. Key indicators include improvement in threat detection rate, shortened incident response time, and decrease in false positive cases.

3.1.4 Organizational Factors (Moderating Variables)

H4 — Knowledge and Skills: Technical understanding and expertise that security teams possess in relation to AI-based tools. Teams with high levels of AI knowledge are better positioned to unlock the full potential of these tools.

H5 — Perceived Usefulness: The degree to which security professionals believe that using AI tools will improve their job performance. When users genuinely believe a tool helps them detect threats faster or reduces false alarms, they are more likely to trust and use it consistently.

H6 — Perceived Ease of Use: The degree to which security professionals believe that using AI cybersecurity tools requires minimal effort. When a tool is perceived as intuitive and user-friendly, people are more likely to adopt it into daily routines and trust it for critical decisions.

Chapter 4

Methodology

4.1 Introduction

This chapter provides an overview of the design and methodology used in the research. The quantitative approach was applied through surveys, allowing collection of opinions and knowledge from participants. This methodology allows comparing different views easily and finding common patterns, such as common concerns of participants or agreement on the speed of AI.

4.2 Research Strategy

4.2.1 Research Approach

This research uses a quantitative research methodology, with emphasis on the use of numbers to observe trends in collected data. Data was collected using a questionnaire distributed on Google Forms. This approach measures the percentage of people who have heard about AI, their cybersecurity awareness levels, and their attitudes towards AI technology.

4.2.2 Research Philosophy

In terms of epistemology, the research uses the positivist approach — knowledge is gained from observations and measurable information. Fixed quantitative questions with a Likert scale ranging from "Strongly Agree" to "Strongly Disagree" help transform subjective information into objective statistics for further calculations. Researchers do not influence respondents' answers — they receive primary information and analyze the resulting numbers.

4.3 Research Methods

4.3.1 Questionnaire-Based Survey

A structured questionnaire was developed using Google Forms covering: demographic questions (age, gender, field of study), and attitude statements towards AI technology in cybersecurity evaluated on a five-point Likert scale ("Strongly Agree" to "Strongly Disagree").

4.3.2 Advantages of Using Google Forms

Google Forms was selected for its speed and convenience. The survey was distributed online, allowing engagement of a considerable number of 250 respondents using an easy-to-share link. Responses are stored automatically and basic charts and summaries are generated in real time.

4.3.3 Limitations of the Method

Limitations include: (1) Closed questions prevented respondents from providing more elaborate explanations; (2) Respondents may have answered quickly without reading all statements carefully; (3) Online distribution means respondents' interpretations could affect results.

4.4 Sample Selection

4.4.1 Target Population

The target population includes people with a certain degree of knowledge or experience in AI and cybersecurity, encompassing university students studying Information Systems, Computer Science, Engineering, and Cybersecurity, as well as IT and cybersecurity professionals. This population is capable of giving knowledgeable views regarding AI use in cybersecurity environments.

4.4.2 Sampling Technique

Convenience sampling was used — a non-probability sampling technique whereby the subjects most easily accessible to the researcher are selected. The questionnaire was distributed through WhatsApp groups consisting of university students and people with knowledge about IT or cybersecurity. While not the best for generalizability, it is adequate for exploratory and descriptive studies.

4.4.3 Sample Size

250 valid responses were collected. Each submission was reviewed to ensure it was finished and that the individual had worked on the questions. Answers that left mandatory questions blank or that resembled random answering (identical answer marked for each question) were eliminated. All 250 retained responses were considered valid.

4.5 Data Collection

4.5.2 Questionnaire Structure

The questionnaire was divided into four sections:

  • Section A (Demographics): Gender, age group, degree type, and field of study.
  • Section B (General Awareness): Four statements on AI awareness and four statements on cybersecurity awareness.
  • Section C (Research Model Constructs): Seven constructs with five Likert-scale statements each — AI Threat Detection, AI Incident Response, Technical Features, Knowledge and Skills, Usefulness, Ease of Use, and Effectiveness.
  • Section D (Overall Opinion): Four statements on general attitudes toward AI in cybersecurity — support, future importance, investment, and adoption recommendation.

4.5.3 Data Collection Procedure

The questionnaire was distributed via WhatsApp to groups containing students and individuals with IT or cybersecurity backgrounds. An introductory paragraph stated that the study was academic, voluntary, anonymous, and expected to take five to seven minutes. Data collection was conducted from November 1, 2025 to May 1, 2026. All responses were saved automatically, and 250 valid and complete responses were identified.

4.6 Data Analysis

4.6.2 Data Analysis Techniques

Analysis was based on descriptive statistics — interpreting data to make summaries and descriptions without statistical inferences. Frequency counts and percentages were computed for demographic variables. Mean scores for statements and constructs were calculated from Likert-scale responses. A mean score > 3.0 indicates overall agreement; < 3.0 indicates disagreement. Frequency distributions were also considered, and charts and graphs were constructed for visualization.

4.6.3 Tools Used for Analysis

Google Forms was the primary tool, automatically generating visual summaries including pie charts and bar charts showing distribution across the five-point Likert scale. SPSS was used for reliability analysis (Cronbach's Alpha) and hypothesis testing (multiple linear regression). Data collection period: December 1, 2025 to May 1, 2026.

Chapter 6

Conclusion and Future Work

6.2 Summary of Findings

Data collection was completed using an online survey on Google Forms with 250 valid responses from students and professionals from technology-related industries. Key findings across all six research areas:

  • Most participants appeared aware of AI and how it is applied in cybersecurity; the largest group (49.6%) came from the IT field.
  • There was a high level of cybersecurity awareness — participants knew typical threats like phishing and malware.
  • Threat detection was positively assessed: participants believed AI helps detect cyber threats faster and with higher efficiency compared to traditional methods.
  • Incident response was similarly assessed positively — AI allows organizations to react faster and automate response processes.
  • Technical factors were generally trusted, though some respondents had doubts about real-life AI performance.
  • Overall Opinion (Section D) had the highest construct mean (3.64), with 64.8% supporting the use of AI in cybersecurity.

6.3 Conclusion

The current research was designed to evaluate how AI-based capabilities are viewed in relation to cybersecurity and their implementation. According to the results, the perception of AI is positive in terms of threat detection and response capabilities. The fact that respondents' positive perception includes both technical factors and organizational aspects like ease of use and usefulness is quite interesting — it implies that even high-performance AI tools can bring less than expected benefits due to problems at the organizational level.

The results confirm that AI-based cybersecurity solutions are not flawless. Along with undeniable benefits, AI raises concerns related to data accuracy and quality, lack of transparency, and other issues. This research proves that the role of AI in improving cybersecurity in educational institutions and organizations in Bahrain is significant but needs to be implemented carefully.

6.4 Contributions and Limitations

Contributions: (1) Local Bahrain data adds novelty — rare in existing GCC-focused literature; (2) Holistic approach combining both technical and organizational perspectives; (3) Student-oriented data provides insight from the next generation of cybersecurity experts.

Limitations: (1) Convenience sampling may have skewed results toward people comfortable with AI; (2) Reliance on self-reported survey data rather than actual behavior; (3) Sample leaned heavily toward students, particularly undergraduate IT students; (4) No experimental testing or real-world case analysis — conclusions are perception-based.

6.5 Recommendations

💼

For IT Professionals

Gain experience with AI-enabled security solutions (SIEM with ML, SOAR). Incorporate human expertise alongside AI systems. Request explainable outputs from AI vendors. Conduct routine performance audits.

🎓

For Cybersecurity Education

Add AI-specific modules to IT and cybersecurity degrees. Use scenario-based learning with simulated attacks. Establish cooperation with companies for internship opportunities. Include ethics and governance in AI courses.

🏛️

For Organizations in Bahrain

Implement a strategic AI adoption plan with clear goals and performance metrics. Develop national-level policies using NIST AI Risk Management Framework. Focus on workforce development initiatives. Facilitate cross-sector collaboration and threat intelligence sharing.

6.6 Future Work

  • Expand the sample to IT managers and employees in banking, healthcare, and government sectors for more comprehensive findings.
  • Move from perception-based surveys to experimental methods — comparing human vs. AI malware detection efficiency.
  • Employ structural equation modeling (SEM) for more precise factor relationship analysis.
  • Conduct longitudinal surveys to see how perceptions change with technology development.
  • Investigate ethical aspects and potential biases of AI systems in cybersecurity contexts.
