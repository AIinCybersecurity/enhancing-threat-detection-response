Abstract

Research Overview

This research project investigates the use of artificial intelligence (AI) in detecting and preventing cyber threats, with a particular focus on the Bahrain context. Cyberattacks are becoming increasingly complex, and manual methods simply are not keeping up — making it crucial to begin using smarter, automated tools.

Two approaches were used: reviewing a substantial body of existing research and conducting a survey with 250 people, primarily IT students and professionals. The goal was to assess whether people are aware of these AI tools, whether they believe they are dependable, and what prompts a company to use these tools.

Results suggest that most respondents have a moderately positive attitude toward AI in cybersecurity. The average attitude score was 3.64 out of 5. Approximately 60% reported that AI is significantly present in cybersecurity today. Concerns remain around AI accuracy and over-reliance. Findings demonstrate that while AI can be highly effective in driving productivity, it is most effective when used as an adjunct to human expertise rather than in isolation.

Chapter 1

Introduction

1.1 Research Background

1.1.1 Overview of Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity is described by NIST (2023) as the process of protecting computers, systems, networks, and data from attacks or any damage, thereby ensuring uninterrupted functioning and safety. With the majority of organizations — including financial institutions, healthcare facilities, educational institutions, and governmental bodies — utilizing computers for various purposes, cybersecurity has become one of the most important tasks within IT.

The primary objective of cybersecurity is to keep the CIA triad: Confidentiality (data being kept private), Integrity (data not being altered without authorization), and Availability (systems and data that are accessible when required). If not addressed, any of these three elements can cause major challenges for an organization.

Cyber threats have evolved and become harder to manage. Phishing attacks, malware, ransomware, and large-scale data breaches are becoming more widespread and damaging. The typical security technologies that depend on known attack signatures and fixed rules are no longer sufficient against these new types of threats. This drives the search for how to use AI in improving cybersecurity.

1.1.2 Evolution of Artificial Intelligence

Early AI systems were mostly rule-based, meaning they had to be programmed with all the logic they required to execute. Simple and structured problems worked exactly as planned, but they failed when there was anything new or unexpected. The introduction of machine learning changed this — enabling systems to learn on their own, steadily and through data, rather than following a set of instructions.

Deep learning followed, utilizing neural networks that process highly complex data such as images, speech, or large amounts of network traffic. Thanks to these developments, AI is far more adaptable and effective in handling rapidly evolving environments — a critical need in cybersecurity. Newer systems can detect unusual patterns even if not previously exposed to such an attack.

1.1.3 Role of AI in Cybersecurity

AI is used for several specific jobs in cybersecurity today. The most apparent is threat detection — AI systems can more quickly and effectively process large amounts of data and identify anything that looks suspicious. This is something that a human analyst wouldn't be able to do in a large organization with thousands of events occurring per second.

AI is also involved in incident response: once a threat is verified, AI can help determine how dangerous it is, and in some instances even automatically respond by isolating the compromised device or blocking suspicious traffic. Additionally, AI helps security teams cut down on false alarms — a long-standing issue where too many alerts are generated, most of which are false.

1.2 Research Problem

Problem Statement: Cybersecurity threats have become more sophisticated, faster, and harder to detect using traditional defence mechanisms. Conventional security systems often rely on static rules, manual monitoring, and reactive responses, making them insufficient against modern attacks such as zero-day exploits, advanced persistent threats (APTs), and large-scale data breaches. While AI offers promising capabilities, such as real-time threat detection, automated response, and predictive analysis, its integration into cybersecurity is not without challenges. Organizations face uncertainties regarding the accuracy, reliability, and ethical implications of AI-based systems, as well as concerns about over-reliance on automation.

This research seeks to:

  • Identify the factors that influence the successful implementation of AI in threat detection and response systems.
  • Assess the impact of these factors on the overall performance, reliability, and decision-making quality of AI-driven cybersecurity solutions.

1.3 Research Questions

  • What are the factors affecting the effective adoption of AI as a threat detection and response tool in cybersecurity?
  • How do these factors influence the capability of AI systems to detect, analyse, and respond to cyber threats?
  • How do organizational knowledge, security team attitudes, perceived usefulness, and perceived ease of use affect the intention to adopt AI-based cybersecurity solutions?
  • How does organizational background, the nature of cybersecurity environments, security preferences, and exposure to cyber threats influence the intention to implement AI technologies for enhanced threat detection and response?

1.4 Research Objectives

  • To understand how modern AI and machine learning improve the speed and accuracy of detecting cyber threats compared to older methods.
  • To explore how AI can help respond to attacks in real time, including learning from new threats as they happen.
  • To study ways to make AI's decisions more transparent and understandable for security teams.
  • To identify challenges like adversarial attacks on AI systems and find ways to make AI-based security stronger and more dependable.

1.5 Research Significance

This research is significant because cyberattacks have increased in frequency and severity. Existing systems rely on fixed signatures or rules for threat detection, which does not work well against new types of attacks. AI — particularly machine learning and deep learning — provides a new approach that can learn over time and detect things that newer attacks may fail to detect through traditional methods.

AI-based IDS systems have been shown to process vast amounts of network traffic and system logs faster than human counterparts, detect suspicious activity faster, and identify both known and unknown attack types by learning normal network behaviors. AI also helps speed up incident response through automated response systems that can quickly contain threats by isolating affected systems or blocking malicious traffic before severe damage is done.

This research adds to the body of knowledge by examining both the technical and human aspects of AI's implementation in cybersecurity. On a practical level, the results can assist Bahrain and other organizations in the Gulf region in making better choices about how to utilize AI within their security systems without sacrificing needed human oversight.

1.6 Gaps Analysis

While there is already significant research around the use of AI in cybersecurity, several gaps remain underrepresented:

  • Much current research focuses on enhancing detection accuracy using ML/DL methods but fails to consider the practical problems of deployment within an organization's environment.
  • There is a lack of explainability and trust — while explainability is a key requirement for AI systems in cybersecurity, much of the existing literature remains highly abstract and not directly applicable to practical solutions.
  • Most studies view threat detection and incident response as two distinct entities, with little research examining AI in an integrated workflow covering detection, response, and continuous learning.
  • There is not enough research that focuses on human and organizational issues — the willingness of security teams to embrace AI, the level of training required, and whether they trust the tools they have been handed.

1.7 Theoretical Framework

1.7.1 AI-Driven Threat Detection Models

Traditional security tools rely on rules or known signatures and are poorly able to defend against attacks they have never seen. AI systems use machine learning and deep learning algorithms that can identify abnormal or harmful activity even when that activity does not match any previously known pattern.

Supervised learning algorithms such as Support Vector Machines or Random Forest have been used to classify network traffic as normal or malicious. Deep learning abilities — including Deep Neural Networks and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) — can establish significantly more intricate patterns concealed in massive datasets. AI models can identify not only known threats, but new and unknown ones as well, including zero-day attacks.

Anomaly detection is another key component: AI can establish a profile of how a network should operate and then look for abnormal activities. This is typically done using unsupervised learning methods such as clustering and auto-encoders, which do not require labeled attacks to function.

AI-powered detection can also aggregate information from various sources — log files, user activity, intrusion detection systems, and endpoint devices — providing a more comprehensive understanding of system events (data fusion) for earlier identification.

1.7.2 Incident Response Frameworks

Incident response encompasses the formal handling process, involving: Preparation, Detection, Containment, Eradication, Recovery, and Post-Incident Review. With AI, all of these stages can be quicker and more accurate.

Based on the NIST Incident Response Life Cycle, AI can automate early stages of detection and prioritize warnings, helping confirm incidents faster. Upon confirmation of a threat, AI systems can take automatic action — isolating infected devices, blocking malicious accounts, and limiting suspicious traffic.

Organizations are now incorporating SOAR (Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response) into their incident response strategies. When integrated with AI, SOAR tools can correlate security alerts from different sources, provide context, and suggest or carry out remediation measures — minimizing critical KPIs such as MTTD (Mean Time to Detect) and MTTR (Mean Time to Respond).

A continuous learning loop is one of the more interesting ideas: once an incident has been resolved, data from the event can be used to refine the AI system to detect and respond better to similar incidents in the future.

1.8 Research Methodology

A mixed approach was used — a combination of quantitative and qualitative methods. The quantitative side was supported with data collection through an online survey using Google Forms, targeted at university students and IT sector workers in Bahrain, using a 5-point Likert scale.

The qualitative component involved analysis of the academic literature, models, and frameworks about AI in cybersecurity, providing theoretical basis and context for the survey results.

1.8.1 Data Collection

Primary data: Google Forms online questionnaire distributed via digital means. Closed-ended questions gauging participants' knowledge of AI use in cybersecurity and trust in automated defenses, rated on a five-point Likert-type scale.

Secondary data: Academic journals and conference papers published from 2016 to 2025, sourced from IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, and SpringerLink. Key areas examined included Machine Learning-based Intrusion Detection, Automated Response Systems, and Explainable AI in Cybersecurity.

1.8.2 Data Analysis

Survey answers were transformed into facts. Background questions were compiled into percentages. Likert-scale questions were analyzed by finding average scores to show whether the group mostly agreed or disagreed. Charts were created to make patterns visible. Common themes were identified from the literature and compared to questionnaire responses.

1.8.3 Ethical Considerations

All data were collected ethically. Participation in the survey was voluntary and anonymous, and no personal or sensitive information was recorded. All secondary sources were properly cited following academic standards.

Chapter 2

Literature Review

2.1 Cybersecurity

2.1.1 Definition of Cybersecurity

As defined by NIST (2023), cybersecurity refers to the processes adopted to safeguard computers, computer networks, and any information stored within such devices against any unauthorized access, destruction, or disruption. Among the core concepts in this area include confidentiality (keeping data confidential), integrity (protecting data from unauthorized modification), and availability (ensuring accessibility of the data).

Cybersecurity goes beyond technology — it includes both policy and behavior within organizations. Even with the most advanced protection software, optimal security cannot be achieved without proper policy and employee conduct.

2.1.2 Importance of Cybersecurity

The significance of cybersecurity has increased tremendously because most organizations now depend on technology to store critical information and conduct their businesses. A cyberattack can lead to devastating effects including financial losses, operational disruption, and legal ramifications. Health organizations, financial institutions, educational organizations, and government agencies are at the highest risk because of the value of their information.

A poor security system creates great risks of being attacked or hacked, which leads to a loss of customer and partner confidence. Given that cyber-attacks are becoming increasingly common and hard to prevent, ignoring cybersecurity issues is no longer an option for enterprises.

2.1.3 Common Cybersecurity Threats

  • Malware: All kinds of malicious programs and software used to corrupt or damage systems, steal confidential information, or break into networks.
  • Phishing: Use of deceptive techniques to trick users into clicking links or sharing passwords.
  • Denial-of-Service (DoS) and DDoS attacks: Flooding a server with too many requests so regular clients cannot connect.
  • Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs): Highly qualified professionals entering a network and remaining there for months to collect and extract information secretly.
  • Insider threats: Actions by users or employees, either intentional attacks or accidental errors.

2.1.4 Incident Response in Cybersecurity

Incident response refers to the sequential approach employed by organizations in reaction to a detected cyberattack. Per NIST (2023), the procedure involves six stages: preparation, detection and analysis, containment, eradication, recovery, and post-incident activity. This is critical for minimizing potential harm while restoring operational capabilities promptly.

Successful incident response requires the utilization of security-related technologies combined with the involvement of human decision-makers. The existing approach is insufficient due to its reliance on manual intervention, which leads to delays in addressing cyberattacks, particularly when they are massive or intricate. This is why scholars are currently focused on exploring AI-driven and automated incident response procedures.

2.1.5 Traditional Cybersecurity Approaches

Conventional security software has been reliable until recently, but cannot cope with contemporary cybersecurity challenges. Most of these applications rely on signature-based detection — if hackers come up with any new malware or change existing malware, the conventional tool will not be able to detect it.

Additionally, conventional security tools are known for excessive alerting — issuing multiple alerts, most of which tend to be false. Security personnel become fatigued after receiving thousands of false alerts daily, making it difficult to pay attention to real attacks. Furthermore, these tools lack interoperability, making it challenging to coordinate security team efforts for threat detection across entire infrastructures.

2.2 Artificial Intelligence (AI)

2.2.1 Definition and Overview

Artificial Intelligence refers to programs written for computers to perform tasks that would normally be accomplished by humans — including problem solving, reasoning, pattern recognition, and many others. The majority of AI used today belongs to the category of "narrow AI" — a particular application that can complete a predetermined task extremely effectively. The key difference between AI and ordinary software is the ability to improve through practice and experience acquisition, constantly updating its knowledge base by acquiring new data.

2.2.2 History of Artificial Intelligence

Early AI was very basic and ran based on predefined rule sets, making it operate like a machine following an algorithm. It could not provide output when input did not align with the rule set. The emergence of machine learning allowed computers to identify patterns in data without being programmed to do so by humans. Today, AI uses deep learning — involving artificial neural networks that mimic some cognitive functions in human beings — to enable interpretation of images, text, and network traffic analysis.

2.2.3 AI Tools and Techniques

Machine learning comprises three main approaches: supervised learning (using pre-labeled data to derive conclusions), unsupervised learning (exploring data and discovering patterns automatically), and reinforcement learning (learning through experiments with rewards for correct actions).

Deep learning is a particular type of machine learning that uses neural networks to work with highly sophisticated data. Researchers rely on designs such as CNNs (Convolutional Neural Networks) and RNNs (Recurrent Neural Networks) to help computers detect images, recognize voice patterns, and identify suspicious activities within a network. Other methods include natural language processing and expert systems.

2.2.4 Applications of AI in Different Fields

  • Healthcare: Helping doctors determine diseases and monitor patient health conditions.
  • Finance and Banking: Detecting fraud, determining loan eligibility, and assessing risks.
  • Transport: Managing traffic and enabling self-driving cars.
  • Manufacturing: Predictive maintenance — diagnosing machines with potential breakdowns before they occur.
  • Cybersecurity: Detecting potential threats, analyzing network behaviors, and stopping cyberattacks automatically.

2.2.5 Factors Impacting the Effectiveness of AI

For an AI model to be effective: (1) A lot of unbiased data must be fed into the model — corrupted data leads to a model that makes mistakes due to bias. (2) Transparency in AI systems — the ability to explain why the AI came to a particular conclusion. Many intelligent machines, especially those employing deep learning, have been referred to as "black boxes" because the reasons for decisions are hard to comprehend, creating mistrust. Other factors include the amount of computing power needed and the number of people running the systems.

2.3 Integration of AI in Cybersecurity

2.3.1 Relationship between AI and Cybersecurity

AI and cybersecurity are linked due to the massive amounts of data produced by computers every day — including logs of internet traffic as well as users' actions on various devices. AI technologies are adept at processing such massive amounts of data, detecting hidden trends as well as unusual behavior.

One key strength of AI for cybersecurity is its ability to adapt to changes. Static rules are characteristic of traditional security methods, making any changes in threats challenging to incorporate. An AI-based security system can learn from each threat and update its defenses accordingly, making it particularly advantageous against zero-day attacks.

2.3.2 AI for Enhancing Threat Detection

AI systems can simultaneously analyze old and current data to detect whether network traffic is legitimate or malicious. Deep learning can find increasingly subtle patterns hidden within large datasets, improving detection results in high-traffic networks where attackers use sophisticated methods to evade detection.

Anomaly detection involves developing a model of typical network traffic and setting up alerts whenever deviation occurs. This method is effective for detecting insider threats and zero-day attacks that regular systems would otherwise overlook. AI operating in real time means threats can be identified much faster — helping contain them quickly while also reducing false positives.

2.3.3 Incident Response and Threat Analysis using AI

AI extends beyond threat detection — it makes important contributions to how companies address problems associated with breaches. The remedy process is usually lengthy and tedious, proving difficult when an attack needs to be contained immediately. AI assists through automation of activities and decision-making, leading to faster action and improved knowledge.

AI also assists in determining the scope of security incidents, correlating incidents to network activities, and implementing automatic actions like blocking malicious traffic and isolating affected systems. Additionally, AI is extremely helpful in retrospective analysis and understanding the root causes of intrusions, which is vital for developing preventive actions for future attacks.

2.3.4 Technical Factors Influencing AI-based Threat Detection

  • Data Quality: For the algorithm to work properly, large amounts of reliable data are required. Inaccurate data may result in flawed conclusions and high false positive rates.
  • Model Complexity: More complex deep-learning algorithms are likely to have better results in terms of threat identification, but may require significant computing resources and could be difficult for security experts to understand, thus lacking their full trust.
  • Robustness: The capability of the system to resist manipulation attempts by an adversary who tries to mislead the AI by presenting carefully chosen inputs. Constant updating of the model is necessary to maintain resilience.

2.3.5 Future Trends in AI-driven Cybersecurity

Current primary focus lies on developing Explainable AI (XAI) — making the rationale behind computations comprehensible to security experts, which will increase confidence among human users. Another area revolves around predictive AI — using past data to predict when and where future attacks might occur. Scientists are also improving AI algorithms for cloud computing and network security.

However, challenges remain: privacy, discrimination, biased algorithms, excessive automation reducing human attention, and the alarming trend of adversaries using AI to conduct more advanced attacks — fueling what some describe as a "digital arms race." Overall, AI is viewed as the best solution for increasing security, but its use must be carefully regulated to ensure safety and ethics.

Chapter 3

Research Model and Hypotheses

3.1 Research Model

This research is based on a model consisting of three main categories of variables:

  • Independent Variables (Technical AI Skills): AI Threat Detection, AI Incident Response, and Technical Features of AI Systems.
  • Dependent Variable: Effectiveness of Cybersecurity Threat Detection and Response.
  • Moderating Variables (Human/Organizational Factors): Organizational Knowledge and Skills, Perceived Usefulness of AI, and Perceived Ease of Use.

3.1.1 Conceptual Research Framework

The central assumption postulates that AI can improve the security situation significantly by helping teams identify risks faster and more accurately, while responding faster to attacks and maintaining efficiency. However, the relationship between AI and security is affected by organizational factors and people. AI will deliver results effectively only when workers have been professionally trained and the usefulness and usability of AI tools are clear to the team.

3.1.2 Independent Variables

AI-based Threat Detection Capabilities: The ability of AI-powered devices to recognize cyber threats using data analysis. Traditional software is limited to identifying previously documented attacks, while AI-powered programs leverage machine learning to conduct data analysis. AI can recognize threats using supervised learning to categorize traffic as safe or malicious, and anomaly detection to identify zero-day attacks and internal threats.

AI-based Incident Response Capabilities: The ability of AI systems to help or react on their own when faced with a threat — including ranking alerts by importance, correlating incidents, and performing automatic actions such as removing infiltrated hosts or blocking suspicious traffic without human input. Quick response is critical for ensuring minimal damage and enabling swift return to normalcy.

Technical Factors of AI Systems: Technical factors form the basis of an effective AI-based security tool. This includes data quality and accuracy, ability to scale, and integration into existing security architecture. AI systems must also withstand adversarial attacks and be seamlessly integrated with other tools to offer any significant advantage.

3.1.3 Dependent Variable

The dependent variable is the Effectiveness of Cybersecurity Threat Detection and Response — the level of capability within a firm to detect any cyber threats and effectively react to security threats to avoid damage and return to regular operations. Signs of effectiveness include improved threat detection rate, shortened incident response time, and decrease in false positives.

3.1.4 Organizational Factors (Moderating Variables)

Organizational Knowledge and Skills: The level of technical understanding and expertise that security teams possess in relation to AI-based cybersecurity tools. Teams with high levels of AI knowledge are better positioned to unlock the full potential of these tools, while untrained teams may mismanage even the most advanced systems.

Perceived Usefulness of AI: The degree to which security professionals believe that using AI tools will improve their job performance. When users genuinely believe a tool helps them detect threats faster or reduces false alarms, they are more likely to trust it and use it consistently.

Perceived Ease of Use: The degree to which security professionals believe that using AI cybersecurity tools requires minimal effort. Tools that are difficult to learn or integrate into existing workflows are unlikely to be used to their full potential, regardless of their technical capabilities.

Chapter 4

Methodology

4.1 Introduction

This chapter provides an overview of the design and methodology used in the research. This chapter is the roadmap of procedures employed to determine the influence of artificial intelligence on improving cybersecurity. A questionnaire survey was used to collect quantitative data — allowing collection of opinions and knowledge from participants effectively.

4.2 Research Strategy

A quantitative strategy was applied through surveys. The quantitative approach was chosen to measure the level of awareness of individuals regarding AI and cybersecurity in an organized manner based on certain scales and standards, allowing consistent data collection and easy comparison among participants.

4.2.1 Research Approach

This research uses a quantitative research methodology, emphasizing the use of numbers to observe trends in collected data. Data was collected via a questionnaire distributed on Google Forms, helping measure the percentage of people who have heard about AI, their cybersecurity awareness levels, and their attitudes towards AI technology.

4.2.2 Research Philosophy

A positivist approach was used in terms of epistemology — meaning knowledge is gained not only through people's subjective experiences or narratives, but from observations and measurable information. Fixed quantitative questions using a Likert scale ranging from "Strongly Agree" to "Strongly Disagree" were utilized to stay objective, transforming subjective information into objective statistics.

4.3 Research Methods

A questionnaire was used for data gathering — the most efficient method when gathering information from a large population in less time. It addressed the level of understanding of AI by individuals, cybersecurity abilities of the respondents, and their views on using AI-based security tools.

4.3.1 Questionnaire-Based Survey

A structured questionnaire was developed using Google Forms with sections covering demographic details (gender, age, education, specialization) and attitudes towards AI technology in cybersecurity using a five-point Likert scale from "Strongly Agree" to "Strongly Disagree".

4.3.2 Advantages of Using Google Forms

Google Forms was selected for its speed and convenience. Since the survey was distributed online, a considerable number of 250 respondents were engaged in no time using an easy-to-share link. Responses are stored automatically with immediate access to base charts and summaries.

4.3.3 Limitations of the Method

  • Closed questions made it impossible for respondents to give more elaborate explanations concerning their responses.
  • Respondents may have answered quickly without reading all statements carefully.
  • Respondents' interpretations might affect results since it was conducted online.

4.4 Sample Selection

The respondents include university students and IT practitioners who are knowledgeable about AI and cybersecurity. Convenience sampling was used as the sampling technique — the questionnaire was sent out via WhatsApp chat groups of university students and IT/cybersecurity personnel. In total, 250 responses were received, all validated and used for analysis.

4.4.1 Target Population

People with a certain degree of knowledge or experience in AI and cybersecurity in the academic or professional sphere — including university students studying Information Technology, Computer Science, Engineering, and Cybersecurity; and IT/cybersecurity professionals. Students studying technical fields are particularly valuable because they are constantly acquainted with emerging changes in AI and cybersecurity throughout their academic training.

4.4.2 Sampling Technique

Convenience sampling was used — a non-probability technique whereby the subjects most easily accessible to the researcher are selected. The questionnaire was distributed through WhatsApp groups consisting of university students and anyone with knowledge about IT or cybersecurity. While not the best sampling from a generalizability perspective, it is perfectly adequate for exploratory or descriptive studies.

4.4.3 Sample Size

250 responses were collected. Each submission was reviewed to ensure it was finished and that the individual had worked on the questions. Answers that left obligatory questions blank, or answers that resembled randomly selected responses (same option marked for each question), were eliminated. Following this review, all 250 responses were validated and retained for analysis.

4.5 Data Collection

Primary data were gathered using an online questionnaire created and administered via Google Forms, from 1st November 2025 to 1st May 2026. The questionnaire consisted of four parts:

  • Part 1 (Demographic): Gender, age range, education, specialization.
  • Part 2 (General Awareness): Statements concerning knowledge of basic principles and cybersecurity threats.
  • Part 3 (Research Model Constructs): Six constructs including AI-based threat detection, AI-based incident response, technical features, knowledge and skills, usefulness, ease of use, and the dependent variable of effectiveness.
  • Part 4 (Overall Opinion): General attitudes towards the application of AI in cybersecurity.

4.5.2 Questionnaire Structure

The questionnaire was categorized into four sections using a five-point Likert scale. Section A covered demographic information. Section B assessed general awareness of AI and cybersecurity through eight statements total. Section C (the largest and most directly related to the research model) had seven constructs, each with five Likert-scale statements. Section D captured general attitudes toward AI in cybersecurity with four statements.

4.5.3 Data Collection Procedure

The questionnaire was distributed via WhatsApp to groups containing students and individuals with IT or cybersecurity backgrounds. An introductory paragraph was provided stating what the study was, that participation was voluntary, that responses would be kept fully anonymous, and that it would take approximately five to seven minutes. There are no correct or wrong answers.

4.5.4 Research Framework

The framework shows how the key factors are interconnected. The independent variables (AI-based Threat Detection, AI-based Incident Response, Technical Features of AI Systems) are expected to directly affect the primary dependent variable: Effectiveness of Cybersecurity Threat Detection and Response. Three supporting variables (Knowledge and Skills, Usefulness of AI, Ease of Use) reflect human and usability aspects that also contribute to understanding outcomes.

4.6 Data Analysis

Analysis consisted of data cleaning, analysis methods, and identification of the research tools used. Responses were screened: those with blank mandatory answers were excluded, and those showing clearly random patterns (same scale option for every question) were also rejected as invalid. All 250 remaining responses were validated.

4.6.1 Data Preparation

Each submission was verified to ensure responses to all mandatory questions were provided. Where responses had been omitted or where there were signs the respondent had not devoted sufficient attention (straight-lining), submissions were removed. A total of 250 responses survived the screening process.

4.6.2 Data Analysis Techniques

Analysis was based on descriptive statistics — interpreting collected data to make summaries and descriptions without statistical inferences. Frequency counts and percentages were computed for demographic variables. Mean scores were computed for Likert-scale questions. A mean score > 3.0 indicates overall agreement; < 3.0 indicates overall disagreement. Frequency distributions were also considered. Charts and graphs were constructed for visualization.

4.6.3 Tools Used for Analysis

Google Forms was the primary tool, automatically generating visual summaries including pie charts and bar charts as responses were collected. Researchers also analyzed data within Google Forms by examining trends in participants' responses and making comparisons across questions. SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) was used for Cronbach's Alpha reliability analysis and multiple linear regression hypothesis testing.

Chapter 6

Conclusion and Future Work

6.1 Introduction

This chapter captures the results from the analysis of the research performed on artificial intelligence for improving the detection of cybersecurity threats. The research was conducted at the University of Bahrain and involved a study to determine how students and IT personnel view the use of AI-based tools for enhancing cybersecurity.

6.2 Summary of Findings

Data collection was completed using an online survey on Google Forms, receiving 250 valid responses from students and professionals from technology-related industries. Questions related to six key areas were asked: awareness of AI, awareness of cybersecurity, application of AI for threat detection, application of AI for incident response, technical factors, and organizational factors.

  • Most participants were aware of what AI is and how it is applied in cybersecurity, seeing it as an advantage against cyber threats. 49.6% came from the IT field.
  • There was a high level of cybersecurity awareness in the sample — participants knew common threats like phishing or malware and claimed to employ measures to protect themselves online.
  • AI technology for threat detection was positively assessed — participants believed AI helped detect threats faster and with higher efficiency compared to traditional methods.
  • AI for incident response also received positive assessments — respondents believed AI allowed organizations to react faster, minimize attack impact, and automate incident response processes.
  • Regarding technical factors, respondents believed AI systems could be trusted and able to process large amounts of cybersecurity data, though some doubts remained about real-life performance.
  • The higher the rating in terms of usefulness and accessibility, the more confidence participants had regarding AI tools' overall effectiveness — consistent with UTAUT theory predictions.

6.3 Conclusion

The research confirms a positive perception of AI technology in terms of threat detection and response capabilities. The fact that respondents' positive perception includes technical factors, as well as important organizational aspects such as ease of use and usefulness, is interesting — it implies that even with high performance capabilities, an AI-based tool can bring less than expected benefits due to organizational-level problems.

The research proves that the role of AI technology in improving cybersecurity in educational institutions and organizations in Bahrain is significant but needs to be implemented carefully, with equal attention to technical and organizational readiness.

6.4 Project Contributions and Limitations

6.4.1 Contributions to the Cybersecurity Field

  • Local data from Bahrain: Adds novelty as such an approach is quite rare in existing research, with practical value for decision makers and educators in the GCC region.
  • Holistic approach: While other authors limit themselves to purely technical assessments, this research explores both technical and organizational dimensions equally.
  • Student-oriented data: In contrast to other empirical works using experts, this research collects data from students who will become the future cybersecurity experts.

6.4.2 Limitations of the Study

  • Sampling method: Distributed through personal and digital networks — not randomly selected, potentially skewing toward people already comfortable with AI and cybersecurity, which may have inflated positive results.
  • Self-reported survey data: Participants reported perceptions and opinions, not actual behavior or experience with real AI cybersecurity systems.
  • Student-heavy sample: Heavily leaned toward undergraduate IT students, limiting applicability to working professionals or non-technical staff.
  • No experimental testing: Conclusions based on perceptions rather than measured performance data — they should be seen as indicative rather than definitive.

6.5 Recommendations

6.5.1 Practical Recommendations for IT Professionals

  • Gain experience working with AI-enabled security solutions such as ML-enhanced SIEM and SOAR features — conduct practical experiments in safe conditions.
  • Incorporate human expertise into work with AI systems — AI solutions require oversight, and humans remain responsible for making security-related decisions.
  • Know how to use AI solutions correctly — understand risks such as adversarial attacks, potential model drifts, and excessive reliance on automation. Conduct routine audits of AI performance.
  • Request that vendors provide AI-enabled tools capable of generating explainable outputs — summaries and explanations of why certain actions were considered.

6.5.2 Academic Recommendations for Cybersecurity Education

  • Educational establishments in Bahrain should add special modules dealing with AI for security — not just introducing ML concepts, but applying them in intrusion detection and log analysis tasks.
  • Prefer scenario learning where students work with simulated attack scenarios and respond using AI cybersecurity tools.
  • Universities should establish cooperation with cybersecurity companies and governmental structures to provide internship opportunities.
  • Ethics and governance should be included in courses focused on AI for cybersecurity applications.

6.5.3 Policy Recommendations for Organizations in Bahrain

  • Implement a clear strategy for adopting and integrating AI cybersecurity solutions — define clear goals, identify security functions that can be automated, and develop performance metrics.
  • Develop national-level policies or guidelines for AI in cybersecurity in Bahrain, based on internationally recognized standards such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework.
  • Focus on workforce development initiatives — providing security professionals with adequate training opportunities and creating career paths, such as professional certifications in AI cybersecurity.
  • Facilitate cross-sector collaborations to discuss cybersecurity threats and AI response strategies, enabling organizations throughout Bahrain to learn from each other.

6.6 Future Work

  • Larger and more varied sample: Future research needs to analyze opinions of IT managers and employees of larger companies in banking, healthcare, and governmental agencies — comparing with student perceptions to understand AI implementation reality in Bahrain.
  • Quantitative experiments: Move from perceptions to actual measurement — compare efficiency of humans vs. AI in detecting malware attacks. Study real-world cases of AI protecting businesses in Bahrain.
  • More complex analytical methods: Employ structural equation modeling to more precisely determine what factors influence attitudes toward AI, increasing mathematical validity.
  • Longitudinal studies: Conduct surveys over time to track how perceptions change alongside the development of technology.
  • Ethical aspects: Future investigations need to pay more attention to ethical aspects and potential biases of AI systems in cybersecurity.
